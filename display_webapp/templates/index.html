    About FlareWatch

    Making air quality data easy to understand for Gulf Coast communities

    FlareWatch turns readings from community air sensors into a dashboard anyone can use. It shows current conditions and past measurements, and it finds and documents spikes in particle pollution in industrial communities along the Gulf Coast.

    Why we're doing this

    People who live next to refineries, chemical plants, and other industrial sites breathe some of the dirtiest air in the country, but they have few ways to check it. Official monitors are expensive and far apart, and the air next to a plant can be very different from the air a few miles away.

    Low-cost PurpleAir sensors help fill that gap, but their raw data is hard to read and harder to act on. Without clear, documented evidence of when and where pollution spikes, it's hard for residents to push for accountability.

    FlareWatch processes that data into a form that's easy to understand, including for older residents and people without a technical background. It's built for residents, Healthy Gulf, and community organizations.

    What FlareWatch does

    NowCurrent air quality at each sensor, on a map and color-coded by the EPA's Air Quality Index
    SpikesAutomatically finds and documents every period above 35 µg/m³, the EPA's 24-hour PM2.5 standard
    HistoryA weekly recap of the worst events, plus the full record of past spikes
    5 minBetween data updates, around the clock

    How it works

    1

    Sensors measure

    Each sensor counts fine particles in the air around it and reports a new reading every couple of minutes.

    2

    We collect the data

    Every 5 minutes, our cloud service on Microsoft Azure gathers the latest reading from every sensor and stores it.

    3

    Spikes are flagged

    As each reading arrives, readings above 35 µg/m³ are grouped into spike events, so nothing is missed between visits.

    4

    Anyone can see it

    The map shows current conditions and the Events tab recaps the past week, translated into the EPA's color-coded Air Quality Index.

    A note on the data

    PurpleAir sensors are low-cost and aren't calibrated the way official regulatory monitors are, so treat their readings as estimates. They're very good at showing when and where air quality changes, but they can read high in humid weather. Temperature is measured inside the sensor's housing and usually reads several degrees warmer than the outdoor air.

    For official air quality forecasts and alerts, visit AirNow.gov ↗.
